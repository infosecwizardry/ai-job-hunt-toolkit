# Professional Summary

Help the person write a resume summary that makes a hiring manager want to talk to them. The summary is their elevator pitch. It sits at the top of the page and gets read first, and most summaries waste that spot.

## Why this matters

Most summaries read like this:

> "Motivated, experienced SOC analyst passionate about protecting organizations and eager to grow my skills."

A hiring manager learns nothing from that. Anyone could send it to any company. It names qualities instead of showing them, so it reads as filler.

A good summary does three jobs:

1. **The role.** States plainly what job they're going after.
2. **The fit.** Makes a specific connection between what they've actually done and this role.
3. **Who they'd be on the team.** Shows what kind of teammate they are through what they do.

The third part is where most people fall short, and it's what hiring managers building a team care about most. They're looking for someone who is self-driven, actively getting after the learning, able to find their own answers, easy to teach, and willing to chip in. They want a motivated person, not a warm body in a seat. Writing "self-driven" doesn't show any of that. A home lab, studying at night, chasing the tickets nobody else could answer, or writing things up for the team does.

## Inputs

Ask for these if they weren't provided:

- **The job listing** they're applying to (required). The summary is written for one listing at a time.
- **Their resume** (required). This can be a resume already tailored to the listing, their master list, or their current resume. When the `resume` skill calls this one, use the approved draft it passes you and replace its `[SUMMARY GOES HERE]` placeholder.
- **Their current summary** (optional), if they want it reworked rather than written fresh.

## Workflow

### 1. Read before asking

Read the job listing and the resume first. Identify the role and the two or three requirements their experience fits best. Don't ask the person anything you can already answer from what they gave you.

### 2. Check what's missing

For each of the three parts, decide whether the resume already gives you enough real material. The role and the fit usually come from the listing and the resume. The "who they'd be on the team" part usually doesn't, because learning and growth often happen outside of a job.

### 3. Interview briefly

Ask only about what's missing, one question at a time. Keep it to about four questions so it doesn't drag. Good questions:

- What are you doing right now to learn and grow, outside of work?
- When you hit a problem nobody had an answer for, what did you do?
- What's something you figured out on your own that you then shared with your team?
- What kind of work do people come to you for?

Always push for a specific example. If they answer with an adjective ("I'm a fast learner"), ask what they did that shows it.

### 4. Write two versions

Write two summaries, each 3 to 4 sentences, so they can pick one or combine them. Under each version, show which sentence covers the role, the fit, and the team part. This teaches the structure so they can maintain it themselves.

### 5. Revise on request

Make changes they ask for. If a change would add a claim they haven't backed up, ask about it before writing it in.

## Writing rules

- **Show, don't claim.** Personality comes from behavior and specifics, never from adjectives about themselves.
- **Cut the filler.** Don't use words like motivated, passionate, detail-oriented, results-driven, hard-working, team player, self-starter, or dynamic. These are the words in every summary that gets skipped.
- **Sound like a person.** Write the way they'd say it out loud, not like a template. Default to first person ("I"), because it carries personality better. If they ask for traditional resume style with no pronouns, switch to that.
- **Skip "why this company"** unless they genuinely tell you a reason. A forced line about the company's mission reads as cheesy and wastes space.
- **Write for this listing.** Mirror the role title and the requirements that matter most in this listing. A summary that would fit any job isn't doing its job.

## Guardrail: only what's true

Use only facts from their resume and their answers. Never invent certifications, tools, numbers, projects, or experience, and never make anything sound bigger than what they told you. If something is unclear, ask.

The reason is simple: everything on the resume is fair game in the interview. If they can't defend a line when an interviewer asks about it, it shouldn't be on the page.
